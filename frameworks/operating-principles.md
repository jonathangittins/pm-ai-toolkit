# PM Operating Principles

*Based on Shreyas Doshi's Good vs Great PM dimensions*

## Core Principle

**The gap between good and great PM work is usually not effort or intelligence – it's operating posture.** Good PMs solve problems well. Great PMs choose which problems to solve, prevent the ones that matter, and make the people around them better.

---

## The Principles

### 1. Ask "What am I getting wrong?" not "Does this make sense?"

"Does this make sense?" invites agreement. "What am I getting wrong?" invites challenge. The second question surfaces blind spots; the first confirms what you already believe.

Apply this to: spec reviews, strategy presentations, 1:1s with engineering leads.

### 2. Prevent problems, don't just solve them

Great PMs are discerning about which problems to prevent, which to solve, and which to leave alone. Problem prevention is invisible work – nobody celebrates the incident that didn't happen – but it's higher leverage than firefighting.

The diagnostic: if you're spending most of your time reacting, you're operating below your level.

### 3. Metrics-informed, not metrics-driven

Use data to inform judgment, not replace it. Metrics-driven means the dashboard decides. Metrics-informed means you weigh quantitative data alongside qualitative signal (customer conversations, support patterns, competitive moves) and make a call.

The difference matters most when metrics conflict with intuition. That's when you need judgment, not a dashboard.

### 4. Stakeholders are advisors, not approvers

If you treat stakeholders as approvers, you optimise for getting sign-off. If you treat them as advisors, you optimise for getting better ideas. The relationship shifts from "please approve my plan" to "what am I missing in this plan?"

### 5. Facilitate ideas, don't just present yours

Good PMs walk into a room with a strong opinion and defend it. Great PMs walk into a room, set up the conditions for the team to generate ideas, and synthesise the best thinking into a direction. The team builds more conviction when they helped shape the answer.

### 6. Ensure strategy is right before optimising execution

A team's time is too precious to spend on impeccable execution of a flawed strategy. Before asking "how do we build this well?", ask "should we be building this at all?" and "is this the highest-leverage thing we could do?"

### 7. The product is the entire customer experience

Docs, API, support, onboarding, error messages – all of it is "the product." If the feature works perfectly but the help article is wrong and the error message is cryptic, you shipped a broken product.

### 8. Share failures widely, not just wins

Good PMs share lessons within their team. Great PMs share failure post-mortems company-wide. This compounds – every team benefits from mistakes they didn't have to make themselves.

### 9. Keep the monkey on the right back

*From Oncken & Wass, "Who's Got the Monkey?" (HBR, 1974)*

Every time someone brings you a problem and you say "let me think about it," the monkey – the next action – jumps from their back onto yours. Accumulate enough monkeys and you're buried in other people's work while they wait idle.

The fix: every conversation ends with the monkey on the other person's back, with a clear next action and a follow-up date.

**Five degrees of initiative** (push people up this ladder):
1. Wait until told
2. Ask what to do
3. Recommend, then act on approval
4. Act, then advise immediately
5. Act independently, report routinely

Most people default to level 2 or 3. Great PMs coach their teams to operate at level 4-5, and operate at level 4-5 themselves when managing up.

**Applies to managing up too.** Don't bring your VP a monkey – bring a recommendation with a clear ask.

### 10. Give away your Legos

*From Molly Graham, "Give Away Your Legos" (First Round Review)*

As your team or company scales, your instinct is to hold onto responsibilities you've mastered. Resist. If you're still doing the same job after the team doubled, you're the bottleneck.

The emotional progression:
1. You have too many Legos (overwhelmed, doing 10 jobs)
2. New people arrive and take some Legos (feels threatening)
3. Your instinct says grab them back or micromanage
4. The right move: find a bigger, better tower to build

This applies directly when handing off product areas. When a new PM takes over a product area, your job isn't to supervise – it's to document what you know, hand over relationships, and go find the next unsolved problem.

## Self-Assessment

Use these as a periodic check (monthly review, quarterly reflection):

| Dimension | Good | Great | Where am I? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decision inputs | Data-driven | Data-informed + qualitative | |
| Problem orientation | Solve well | Prevent or choose not to solve | |
| Stakeholder posture | Seek approval | Seek advice | |
| Idea generation | Present strong opinions | Facilitate team thinking | |
| Strategy vs execution | Execute well | Validate strategy first | |
| Product scope | Feature works | Entire experience works | |
| Knowledge sharing | Team-level | Company-wide | |
| Leverage | Do high-quality work | Delegate and multiply | |
| Domain expertise | Well-researched | Becoming a world expert | |
| Delegation | Accept problems from others | Keep the monkey on the right back | |
| Scaling | Hold onto what you know | Give away your Legos, find bigger problems | |

## Integration with Existing Frameworks

### Connection to Communication Guidelines
Principle #1 ("What am I getting wrong?") is a communication technique. Apply it alongside the hooks and active voice rules.

### Connection to LNO Effectiveness Framework
Principle #2 (problem prevention) maps to Leverage tasks in LNO. Prevention work is almost always Leverage – high impact, compounds over time, nobody else will do it.

### Connection to Inversion Framework
Principles #2 and #6 connect directly to inversion thinking. "How could this strategy be wrong?" is both an inversion question and a great PM operating question.

## Anti-Patterns

- ❌ **Solving every problem that lands on your desk** – Some problems solve themselves. Some aren't worth solving. Triage ruthlessly.
- ❌ **Conflating busyness with impact** – Responding to every Slack thread is not high-leverage work.
- ❌ **Optimising for stakeholder comfort** – Getting easy approvals feels productive. Getting hard feedback is productive.
- ❌ **Being the smartest person in the room** – If you're always the one with the answer, you're not multiplying.
