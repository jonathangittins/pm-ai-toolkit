# Prospect Theory for Product Management

*Based on Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's Prospect Theory (1979), which won Kahneman the Nobel Prize in Economics (2002).*

## Core Principle

**People don't evaluate outcomes rationally. Losses feel roughly twice as painful as equivalent gains feel good.** This creates systematic biases in product decisions – teams over-protect what exists and under-invest in what could be.

---

## The Three Biases That Matter for PM

### 1. Loss Aversion – "Removing feels 2x harder than not building"

Sunsetting a feature that 50 users rely on feels like a bigger decision than not building something 500 users would benefit from. The emotional weight is disproportionate to the actual impact.

**How it shows up:**
- Features with minimal adoption stay in the product because removal feels like a loss
- Instead of sunsetting, teams invest in "making it more visible" or "giving it one more chance" – compounding the sunk cost
- Vocal minority users (even one per organisation) create disproportionate gravitational pull

**The diagnostic:** If you're investing in increasing adoption of something that's been available for months with low uptake, ask whether you'd build it from scratch today knowing what you know. If the answer is no, the effort to revive it is loss aversion, not strategy.

### 2. Sunk Cost – "We've already spent 4 weeks on this"

Time already invested becomes a reason to keep going, even when new information says to stop. The 4 weeks are gone regardless of what you do next – but writing them off *feels* like a loss.

**How it shows up:**
- Features that should be descoped mid-build get finished because "we're almost there"
- Projects that have overrun their estimates keep getting extended rather than re-evaluated
- "We've come this far" becomes an argument in planning discussions

**The diagnostic:** When deciding whether to continue a project, imagine you're starting fresh today with no prior investment. Would you choose to start this project now, given everything else competing for the same resources?

### 3. Anchoring – "The first number sticks"

The first estimate, target, or framing creates a reference point that's hard to shift. All subsequent discussion orbits that anchor.

**How it shows up:**
- An early engineering estimate becomes the assumed timeline even as scope evolves
- The first proposed solution shapes the design space, making alternatives feel like departures rather than options
- Revenue targets set in one context persist even when market conditions change

---

## When to Use This Framework

- **Reviewing the "later" bucket** – are items there because of genuine future value, or because saying "no" felt like a loss?
- **Sunset decisions** – recognising that the emotional difficulty of removal is disproportionate to the actual user impact
- **Mid-build go/no-go decisions** – separating sunk cost from forward-looking value
- **Prioritisation discussions** – noticing when a vocal minority or a senior stakeholder's preference is anchoring the conversation

---

## The Two Diagnostic Questions

For any feature on your roadmap, especially in "later" or "researching" states:

| Question | What it reveals |
|----------|----------------|
| **Do you know who's asking for it and why?** | Safe bets have names, accounts, and data points. Middle-ground items have vague provenance – "customers have mentioned it" or "it just makes sense." |
| **What happens if you don't do it?** | Safe bets have an articulable cost of inaction (lost deals, churn, support load). Middle-ground items – nothing bad happens. Nobody churns. The product is slightly less polished, but the world moves on. |

If an item fails both questions, it's occupying mental real estate and planning bandwidth without earning it. It's a candidate to cut or demote – and the discomfort you feel about cutting it is itself prospect theory in action.
